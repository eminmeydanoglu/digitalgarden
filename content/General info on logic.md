### argument 

The notes then provide a formal definition for the structure of a proof:

> **"Definition: Any implication (i.e. $A \to B$) is called an argument. A general form of an argument is of the form**
> 
> **$(P_1 \land P_2 \land \dots \land P_n) \to Q \quad (*)$"**


IF $P_1$ is true, AND $P_2$ is true, AND... AND $P_n$ is true, THEN $Q$ must be true.
We say that the argument $(*)$ is valid if $(P_1 \land P_2 \land \dots \land P_n) \to Q$ is a tautology. An argument is **valid** if its logical structure is perfect. It means that it is _impossible_ for all the premises to be true and the conclusion to be false simultaneously.


Denote the argument as:

> $$\begin{array}{c} P_1 \\ P_2 \\ \vdots \\ P_n \\ \hline Q \end{array}$$


For any (compound) statements $A$ and $B$, if the implication $A \to B$ is a tautology, then we say that A logically implies B and we write $A \implies B$.

"Note that '$\to$' and '$\implies$' are different! ($P \implies Q$ means that $P \to Q$ is a tautology)"
